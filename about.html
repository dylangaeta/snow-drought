What this is

A public, data-forward explorer built on top of snowdrought-carbon, a private NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory / CIRES research codebase for studying winter and snow drought across the Western US. This site is not an official NOAA or NIDIS product, issues no forecasts, and makes no causal or year-against-year claims — every chart and map is a direct, descriptive read of the underlying computed values.

How anomalies are computed

Every anomaly on this site is the departure from that calendar month's own baseline average — the baseline period is the later of 1990 or that product's own record start, through 2024. Native standardized indices (SPI, SPEI, EDDI, PDSI, ForDRI, and ESI) are already their own standardized departure statistic at the source and are shown directly rather than recomputed.

On the summary table, five ways to display the same underlying anomaly are available:

    Cross-product views

    Three tools on this site overlay or aggregate more than one product at once. All three are computed live in the browser from the same per-product JSON everything else on this site reads — nothing is a static image, and no product is ever averaged or blended into another.

    • Compare (Compare): every product that shares a physical theme (snow, soil moisture, vegetation, ...) overlaid on one standardized-anomaly (σ) axis, drawn solid for an observation and dashed for a model or data-driven product; check a variable to add it to the chart. Vegetation responses are shown only during each product's own active growing season — a dormant-season baseline near zero would otherwise make a tiny absolute anomaly explode when standardized. Every series is sign-flipped by its own stress direction before overlay (the same convention the Summary Table, Heatmap, and Map use), so positive always reads as the stress direction regardless of a variable's raw sign convention.
    • Heatmap (Heatmap): every product's standardized anomaly, one row per product, for any month or season window back to 1990, as one color matrix per category — gold always means stress and teal always means relief, regardless of a variable's own raw sign convention.
    • Dierauer classification (Classification): each DJFM winter placed in anomaly space across precipitation, temperature, snowpack, VPD, and snow-cover, classified dry / warm-and-dry / warm / no snow drought following Dierauer, Allen & Whitfield (2019, Water Resources Research).

    U.S. Drought Monitor categories

    USDM values on this site are the drought classification (0–4, corresponding to D0–D4) blended with the underlying percentile. Real-world impact descriptions below are quoted directly from drought.gov's own category explainer.

    CategoryPercentileTypical impacts (drought.gov)
    D0 — Abnormally Dry20.01–30.00Going into drought: “short-term dryness slowing planting, growth of crops or pastures.” Coming out of drought: “some lingering water deficits” and “pastures or crops not fully recovered.”
    D1 — Moderate Drought10.01–20.00“Some damage to crops, pastures,” “streams, reservoirs, or wells low, some water shortages developing or imminent,” “voluntary water-use restrictions requested.”
    D2 — Severe Drought5.01–10.00“Crop or pasture losses likely,” “water shortages common,” “water restrictions imposed.”
    D3 — Extreme Drought2.01–5.00“Major crop/pasture losses” and “widespread water shortages or restrictions.”
    D4 — Exceptional Drought0.00–2.00“Exceptional and widespread crop/pasture losses” and “shortages of water in reservoirs, streams, and wells creating water emergencies.”

    Data availability

    Observing system record length and latency

    Actual local temporal coverage for every product, colored by category. Bar length is real record coverage, not an estimate -- computed directly from this dashboard's own manifest.

    Gaps in an individual product's own record are not shown here (see that product's own page for its actual monthly coverage) -- this view is about which products reach which dates at all.
