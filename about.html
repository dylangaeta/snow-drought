About this dashboard

This is an open, public tool for exploring and learning from winter and snow drought across the Western US — a data-exploration and educational resource, first and foremost. It brings dozens of openly available climate, snow, hydrology, and carbon datasets together in one place and presents each as a plain, descriptive view of the numbers: interactive maps, time series, seasonal cycles, and summary tables you can explore, compare, and download figures from. Every chart and map is a direct read of the selected variables and datasets.

How anomalies are computed

Every anomaly on this site is the departure from that calendar month's own baseline average — the baseline period is the later of 2000 or that product's own record start, through 2024. The SNOTEL station views compare each day against that station's own 1990–2024 median. Native standardized indices (SPI, SPEI, EDDI, ForDRI, and ESI) are already their own standardized departure statistic at the source and are shown directly rather than recomputed. Index-scale measures (PDSI, VegDRI, USDM) are shown on their own native scale and are re-standardized against their own baseline only for the cross-product σ comparison views.

On the Summary Statistics page, several ways to display the same underlying anomaly are available:

    Cross-product views

    Three tools on this site place more than one product in a single view. All three are computed live in the browser from the same per-product JSON everything else on this site reads — nothing is a static image, and no product is ever averaged or blended into another.

    • Compare (Compare): any two variables on one shared time axis — in raw units each reads on its own y-axis (A left, B right), or switch to σ to standardize both onto one axis. The σ here is native-direction (each variable against its own baseline), not the stress-direction flip the Heatmap and Map use.
    • Heatmap (Heatmap): every product's standardized anomaly, one row per product, for any month or season window back to 1990, as one color matrix per category — brown always means more stress and teal always means less stress, regardless of a variable's own raw sign convention.
    • Dierauer classification (Classification): each DJFM winter placed in anomaly space across precipitation, temperature, snowpack, VPD, and snow-cover, classified dry / warm-and-dry / warm / no snow drought following Dierauer, Allen & Whitfield (2019, Water Resources Research).

    Data availability

    Observing system record length and latency

    Actual local temporal coverage for every product, colored by category. Bar length is real record coverage, not an estimate -- computed directly from this dashboard's own manifest.

    Gaps in an individual product's own record are not shown here (see that product's own page for its actual monthly coverage) -- this view is about which products reach which dates at all.

    Colors, maps & software libraries

    • Color tables: NCAR Command Language (NCL) color tables, sampled via the open-source cmaps package — see the NCL Color Table Gallery (UCAR/NCAR).
    • Basemap: interactive map tiles © OpenStreetMap contributors, under the Open Database License (ODbL).
    • Interactive maps & charts: OpenLayers with geotiff.js for Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF rendering; Plotly.js for time-series and scatter charts.
    • Geospatial processing: GeoPandas, Shapely, xarray, and rasterio. State boundaries from Natural Earth; HUC basins from the USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset.

    Disclaimers

    • This site is not an official NOAA, NIDIS, or U.S. Drought Monitor product. It issues no forecasts and makes no causal or year-against-year claims — every chart and map is a direct, descriptive read of the selected variables and datasets.
    • For data access, authoritative values, complete metadata, quality flags, version history, and terms of use, always defer to each dataset's original provider (linked in the Datasets & references table above). The versions shown here are reprocessed for visualization and may lag, be cropped to the Western US, differ in spatial resolution, or apply different pre-processing and QA-flag handling of satellite layers than the source.
